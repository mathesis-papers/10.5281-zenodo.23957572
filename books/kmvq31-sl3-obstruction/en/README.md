# Why can eight directions not fit?

Suppose two symbols $u,e$ obey the multiplication table

$$
u^2=u,\qquad e^2=u,\qquad ue=0.
$$

Then $(au+be)(cu+de)=(ac+bd)u$. We are not using ordinary multiplication of numbers: choosing the multiplication table is part of choosing the object. It is not even associative, since $(ue)e=0$ while $u(e^2)=u$. Yet we can ask a familiar question: which invertible changes of coordinates preserve this table? Replacing $e$ by $-e$ works; an arbitrary rotation of the two coordinates need not work.

This is an **evolution algebra**: a vector space with a bilinear multiplication and a basis whose distinct vectors multiply to zero. Such a basis is called a **natural basis**. The name does not require a time evolution here. Every algebra under discussion is finite-dimensional; it need not be associative or have an identity.

Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang start with a more ambitious question: can any prescribed algebraic symmetry group be the **entire** symmetry group of some evolution algebra? This is Question 31 in the survey by Kaygorodov, Martín González and Viruel (original survey §2.2.5, page 24), attributed there to Costoya and Viruel. The paper answers no. Its first special linear obstruction is $\mathrm{SL}_3$, the group of invertible $3\times3$ complex matrices with determinant one. The paper does not claim that this is the smallest counterexample among all groups.

## From full symmetries to tiny symmetries

An invertible linear map preserving multiplication is an **automorphism**. To study these maps, first consider an infinitesimal change $I+\epsilon D$, where $\epsilon^2=0$. Substitute it into multiplication preservation and compare the coefficients of $\epsilon$. The result is

$$
D(xy)=D(x)y+xD(y).
$$

This is the product rule from differentiation, so $D$ is called a **derivation**. All derivations form a vector space $\operatorname{Der}(A)$. With the bracket $[D,E]=DE-ED$, it is a Lie algebra. The bracket records the difference between performing two tiny changes in opposite orders.

For $\mathrm{SL}_3$, expand the determinant in the same way:

$$
\det(I+\epsilon X)=1+\epsilon\operatorname{tr}(X).
$$

Its infinitesimal symmetries are therefore the trace-zero matrices, denoted $\mathfrak{sl}_3$. Nine matrix entries satisfy one independent linear equation, leaving dimension eight. These are the “eight directions” of our title.

## Multiplication forces orthogonal blocks

A symmetric bilinear form $b(v,w)$ is a pairing linear in each variable and unchanged when the variables are exchanged. On the real plane, $b(v,w)=v_1w_1+v_2w_2$ is the dot product. Its infinitesimal preserving transformations satisfy

$$
b(Xv,w)+b(v,Xw)=0.
$$

For the standard dot product this says $X^t=-X$. Such $m\times m$ matrices form the orthogonal Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}_m$. The diagonal entries vanish and each opposite pair of entries is determined by one number, giving dimension $m(m-1)/2$.

The paper proves that a **semisimple** derivation Lie algebra of an evolution algebra must be a finite direct sum of these orthogonal algebras, with every block size $m\ge3$. A direct sum consists of independent blocks working side by side. Semisimple means having no nonzero solvable ideals. An ideal is a subspace closed under brackets with the whole Lie algebra; solvable means that repeatedly taking the space generated by internal brackets eventually gives zero. On a first reading, think of semisimplicity as removing certain layers of internally reducible symmetry. The next chapter states exactly how it is used.

The algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ is semisimple. If it came from an evolution algebra, its eight dimensions would have to be assembled from orthogonal blocks. The small nonzero possibilities are

| $m$ | $\dim\mathfrak{so}_m$ |
|---|---:|
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 6 |
| 5 | 10 |

A ten-dimensional block is already too large. Any sum of threes and sixes is divisible by three, so it cannot be eight. This is not a failed search through a few examples: it is a structural obstruction applying to every finite-dimensional evolution algebra.

::: {.theorem #sl3-obstruction title="The $\mathrm{SL}_3$ obstruction" mathesis="collected:kmvq31-sl3-obstruction:2:s1" status="written-proof" proof="pf-sl3-obstruction" doi="10.5281/zenodo.23957573" source="Theorem 1" request="https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-requests/issues/101"}
For every finite-dimensional complex evolution algebra $A$, $\operatorname{Der}(A)\not\cong\mathfrak{sl}_3(\mathbb C)$ as Lie algebras. Consequently its full automorphism group scheme is not isomorphic to $\mathrm{SL}_3$, and its full automorphism algebraic group is not $\mathrm{SL}_3$. There is no extra assumption on $A^2$ or $\operatorname{Ann}(A)$.
:::


The structural restriction is established in the [orthogonal-block classification](01-proof.md#classification); the eight-dimensional contradiction and the passage from derivations to groups are proved in the [full obstruction argument](02-consequences.md#pf-sl3-obstruction). No assumption on $\dim A^2$ or the annihilator is needed.

## What else does the argument reveal?

The paper does more than exclude one group. It proves the converse to the semisimple classification: every permitted orthogonal sum really occurs as a derivation algebra. When the annihilator is zero, it also classifies the semisimple quotient of an arbitrary derivation algebra, excluding $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ and $\mathfrak g_2$ as simple ideals. Nevertheless, $\mathfrak{sl}_3$ can act faithfully as a subalgebra. Being the whole symmetry algebra, being an ideal, and being a subalgebra are different demands.

Our opening two-dimensional multiplication table gives another contrast: it has only two automorphisms but infinitely many natural bases, even after allowing permutations and independent rescalings. A finite symmetry group does not imply finitely many natural coordinate systems. ## Which boundaries matter?

Isomorphisms of algebraic groups or group schemes retain the tangent structure needed to pass from full symmetries to derivations. An abstract isomorphism of groups of complex points does not automatically give a tangent map. The abstract-group clause of Question 31 is outside the paper's conclusions. Nor does the paper claim that $\mathrm{SL}_2$ or $\mathrm{SL}_4$ occurs as a full automorphism group: their Lie algebras occur. Positive characteristic and general solvable or nilpotent derivation realization also remain outside the results.
