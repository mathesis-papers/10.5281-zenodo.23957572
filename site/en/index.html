A negative answer to Question 31 (section 2.2.5, page 24, arXiv:2610.22248v1): special linear obstructions for evolution algebras

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can every algebraic symmetry group arise as the complete symmetry group of an algebra with a particularly sparse multiplication? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer no for finite-dimensional complex evolution algebras, excluding most special linear groups. Their reason is a classification of the infinitesimal symmetries compatible with this multiplication.

An evolution algebra has a basis in which distinct basis vectors multiply to zero; their squares determine the remaining products. Associativity and an identity are not required. Automorphisms are invertible linear maps preserving multiplication; derivations are infinitesimal symmetries satisfying the product rule D(xy) = D(x)y + xD(y). Derivations form a Lie algebra using the commutator DE − ED. The realization question appears in Kaygorodov–Martín González–Viruel’s survey, attributed to Costoya and Viruel. It distinguishes abstract groups from algebraic groups and group schemes, which retain geometric structure. Following Elduque–Labra’s tangent-space viewpoint, the authors exploit that isomorphisms of the latter structures induce isomorphisms of their derivation Lie algebras.

A symmetric bilinear form is a scalar-valued pairing, linear in each vector and unchanged on exchanging them; nondegeneracy means no nonzero vector pairs to zero with everything. Its infinitesimal preserving transformations form the orthogonal Lie algebra so_m, where m is the underlying dimension. The authors classify semisimple derivation Lie algebras: those without nonzero solvable ideals, where solvability means repeated formation of commutators eventually gives zero. Over any algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, the possibilities are exactly finite direct sums of so_m with m at least three; the zero algebra is allowed. This proves the survey’s Conjecture 5 and answers exactly the semisimple clause of Question 36.

The proof explains why orthogonal structure appears. Semisimplicity first forces the annihilator—the vectors multiplying everything to zero—to vanish: otherwise maps into it yield a forbidden solvable ideal or a derivation with forbidden nonzero trace. Group basis vectors whose squares are proportional, then rescale so their squares agree within each group. The product rule prevents derivations from mixing groups and forces opposite off-diagonal entries within each group. Semisimplicity removes the scalar diagonal terms. Each group therefore carries a skew operator required to kill specified projections of the common squares. These pointwise stabilizers map onto orthogonal Lie algebras with nilpotent kernels, which semisimplicity eliminates.

For SL_n, the determinant-one group, the infinitesimal algebra sl_n consists of trace-zero matrices. The dimension of sl_3 is eight, whereas the permitted orthogonal summands small enough to contribute have dimensions three or six: no sum gives eight. Comparing ranks and dimensions excludes every n at least five as well. The exceptions sl_2 and sl_4 occur as derivation algebras through their identifications with so_3 and so_6; this does not realize SL_2 or SL_4 as full groups. Infinitesimal multiplication preservation gives precisely the derivation rule, transferring these obstructions to group schemes. Cartier’s characteristic-zero smoothness theorem transfers them to algebraic groups, but not to abstract group isomorphisms.

Existence uses the survey’s earlier orthogonal construction. Adjoin a vector u to a symmetric space V, make u idempotent and its products with V zero, and set vw = b(v,w)u, where b is the form. The full automorphism group is its isometry group; direct sums realize the classified derivation algebras. The two-dimensional example u² = e² = u, ue = 0 has only two automorphisms, yet u + te and −tu + e form a natural basis for every positive integer t. Different parameters remain inequivalent under permutations and independent rescalings. Crediting earlier work for the algebra and group, the authors settle the finite-group, zero-annihilator existence clause of Question 14 in minimal dimension two.

For zero-annihilator algebras, the authors also show that removing the derivation algebra’s largest solvable ideal leaves an orthogonal semisimple quotient. This excludes sl_3 and the exceptional Lie algebra g_2 as simple ideals, not as subalgebras: sl_3 acts faithfully on a three-dimensional space and its dual, preserving their symmetric pairing and embedding in so_6. Conversely, every linear map is a derivation of the three-dimensional zero-product algebra, whose derivation algebra contains the simple ideal sl_3. Thus the annihilator restriction matters.

These are written proofs, not extrapolations from finite tests. The manuscript reports Lean evidence for the semisimple classification, the SL_3 tangent bridge and group-scheme obstruction, and the two-dimensional basis-existence statement, not its minimality. The quotient and higher-n conclusions are written proofs. Computations sample orthogonal dimensions through seven, rank comparisons through n = 100, and basis parameters through 25; they do not establish unbounded conclusions. The abstract-group question, full-group realizations of SL_2 and SL_4, positive characteristic, and solvable or nilpotent realization remain outside the results.
